Prop Edge Engine · methodology

How the picks are made

The idea in one sentence: find player performances that are achievable, judged by what the player has done and what he should do against this opponent, where the book's odds are more favorable than that performance deserves, and then combine those favorable bets into parlays for a higher return.

Written September 4, 2026. Back to the Week 1 picks.

1. What we are trying to do

This is not a hunt for mispriced odds for their own sake. The question we ask of every player, every week, is: what is he likely to do in this game, and is the book paying us more than that performance deserves? "Likely to do" rests on two things: what he has done, and what he should do against this opponent, this defense, this game. When the answer is yes, that bet is favorable. Favorable bets from different games are then combined into parlays, because that is how a set of individually sound bets turns into a meaningful return.

So the order of work is: achievable performance first, price second, parlay third. The engine does the arithmetic on the first two; a person checks the football; Monday's scoreboard tells us how well we judged all three.

2. Where our numbers come from

We have six seasons of every NFL play. From that, before every game, we build a picture of each offense and then play the game out thousands of times on a computer. Each replay decides how many plays the team runs, how many are passes, who gets the ball, whether it is caught, how far it goes, who scores. Count how often a player clears a number across all the replays and you have our chance for that bet.

What goes into the replays:

  • The player's own record. How often he is on the field for passes, how often he gets the ball, what he does with it, how many carries he gets. Recent games count more than old ones. A player's first weeks with a new team lean on last season until real games arrive.
  • The game. His team's pace, the total and the point spread, home or away, night game, indoors or out.
  • The weather. The forecast for the kickoff hour: rain takes about 6 yards off a receiver's day, wind over 15 mph shortens the passing game. Indoor games have no weather term.
  • The defense. What the opponent allowed last year and so far this year, measured five ways: yards per target, catch rate, passing touchdowns per throw, yards per carry, rushing touchdowns per carry. We tested how much defense really carries from week to week and the answer is: less than people think. So it moves the number only a little, and every card prints the actual figures and the rank so you can see it. Not yet in: how the defense plays, such as how often it blitzes or whether it leans on man or zone coverage. That is the next build (see section 8).
  • Who is throwing. The quarterback expected to start. If he is new, games under the old one count half.
  • Missing teammates. Games where a key teammate sat count less, so a receiver's numbers are not inflated by a month when the star was hurt.
  • The whole offense at once. A quarterback's passing yards are the sum of his receivers' yards in the same replay, and a back's carries fall in the replays where the passing volume drops. That is what lets us price two bets from one game together honestly.

What we deliberately leave out: a player's past games against this particular opponent (one or two games, mostly noise), and hunches.

3. How we read the book

DraftKings lists each bet as a ladder: 50 yards or more at one price, 60 or more at another, and so on. We capture every rung of every ladder for every game, anonymously, with no account. Each price converts to "how often the book says this hits." Our number sits next to it. The gap between the two is the only reason to bet.

5. How a bet becomes a pick

  1. Our number must be clearly higher than the book's. At least 3 percentage points, and only on bets the book prices between about 35% and 75%. Very long shots and near-certainties are excluded because the engine has shown no skill there.
  2. The player must have actually done it. Last season he cleared that exact number at least as often as the price says, over six or more games. No exceptions.
  3. No red flags. Not on a new team, not with a new quarterback, not a record built while a star teammate was out. Those players are shown but never picked.
  4. The worst case. Every pick is priced a second time with the opposing defense repeating last season in full. A Play has to keep at least 10 points of edge in that worst case. This is what kept Stafford and Pollard on the Week 1 sheet and moved Maye against Seattle to a Lean.
  5. Defenses like this one. Every pick also shows the eight defenses most like the opponent last season, the player's games against them, and how often players in his position reached the number against that group.
  6. The defense check. Every pick prints what the opponent allowed last year, the league average, and the rank. If the opponent is among the eight toughest for that kind of bet, the pick survives only if our edge is at least 5 points. That rule is why Meyers stayed a Play against Cleveland and St. Brown did not against New Orleans.
  7. The football check. A person reads the injury report and the news: trades, roles, what the coaches and beat writers are saying. This is written into the pick as bullets so you can see the reasoning, not just the number.

The engine reads its own numbers two ways and the page shows both. Biggest gap: for each player, the bet where our number is furthest above the book's. Surest favorable bet: for each player, the bet we think hits most often (at least 60 times in 100) whose price is still on our side by 3 or more points. The second list is the one parlays are built from, because each leg is likely on its own and the price is still favorable; the slip's total row says whether the whole thing lands more often than not by our numbers.

Then one of three words: Play (we would bet it), Lean (we like it but something real gets in the way; not bet, out of parlays), Pass (the price is fair or too short). The engine grades every player on its own first; the sheet is what survives the football check. When the two disagree, the page shows both.

6. Parlays

  • Only from Plays. A Lean never goes in.
  • Legs from different games multiply exactly, so the price we show is the price DraftKings will show. Legs from the same game get repriced by the book, and we say so.
  • Every added leg multiplies the payout and the chance of losing alike. Our edge multiplies too, but so does our error: if we are a few points too optimistic on every leg, a five-leg slip is wrong by a lot more than a single. A slip that hits once in a hundred weeks also teaches us nothing on Monday. So the singles are the bet, the two- and three-leg combinations are the parlay, and the long ones are tickets.

7. The weekly rhythm

  • Friday, 4 pm ET. The league's injury report. At 4:30 pm ET the engine captures every DraftKings ladder, reruns the numbers, logs every bet it priced, and rebuilds the page. A person then reviews the picks and publishes.
  • Sunday, before kickoff. A closing capture, so we know where the market ended up.
  • Monday. Every bet the engine priced on Friday, thousands of them, is scored against the result and against the closing price. That score, not the picks, is the real product. Over a season it tells us whether the engine beats the book, and where.

8. What we know we do not know

  • No proven skill in Week 1.
  • Injury reports are checked by hand this season until the automatic feed is live.
  • Only the over side of each line is captured; under prices are not shown.
  • A quarterback's number depends on how many of his receivers the book lists; we now capture the full list every week to keep that stable.
  • Defense is in the numbers as what it allows, but not yet as how it plays. Blitz rate and pass-rusher counts are available play by play from 2022 on and are the next term to build and test: a receiver who wins against the blitz, or a quarterback who does not, is exactly the kind of matchup this engine is meant to find. Man-versus-zone coverage tendencies are not in any free data set; adding them means a paid charting source.
  • Every claim on a card is a number with a source. If a number is wrong we say so on the page and fix it, as we did with Seattle's touchdown rank.

Things we measured and left out

Every term on this page had to earn its place in a test on three seasons it had never seen. These did not, and knowing that is worth as much as the terms that did:

  • A defensive starter being out. Across 3,200 games from 2020 to 2025, a defense missing a full-time corner, safety or linebacker allowed the same yards per pass attempt and the same completion rate as with him, within a percent. Touchdowns per attempt rose a few percent with a full-time corner or safety out, a faint signal, not enough to move a price. The football check still reads the injury report, but "their corner is out" is not the edge it feels like.
  • A player's own record against the blitz. How a quarterback or receiver did against blitzes one year says nothing about the next. The blitz term uses the defense's tendency and the league-wide effects only.
  • Home field in passing. Near zero in the seasons the model was tuned on, about 4 percent in the last three, and Seattle's defense has been easier to throw on at home than away. No separate term.
  • A player's history against one particular team. One or two games; noise.
  • Red-zone share for touchdowns. A receiver's share of his team's red-zone targets is a real, stable trait, but adding it to the touchdown rate we already use did not improve touchdown predictions in the test, so it is out. For backs, red-zone carry share predicts nothing about next year's touchdowns per carry, which barely persist at all.
  • Usage when trailing, and on third down. Neither adds anything to predicting catches beyond a player's overall share of targets.
  • Offensive linemen out. Measured: a missing full-time lineman costs about 1.4% of yards per attempt and does not raise sacks. Small and real; it will be tested as a term once the season's injury feed is live.
  • Deep alternate lines. The engine shows no skill on very long shots or near-certainties, so picks stay between about 35 and 75 percent by the book.

Words on the page

  • Book says: how often the price says the bet should hit.
  • Model says: how often we think it hits.
  • Edge: the gap between the two, in percentage points.
  • 2025 record: how often the player actually cleared that number last season.
  • $10 returns: what a $10 bet pays back including the stake.
  • Product: the leg prices multiplied together, which is exactly what a cross-game parlay pays.
